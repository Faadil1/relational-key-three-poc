# Candidate A — Aotearoa / Māori Hika Ahi

Pair:
- `HIKA` — pointed fire-making stick
- `KAUAHI` — grooved base stick

Authoritative mechanism:
Te Papa describes the hika being rubbed back and forth within the groove of the kauahi to generate heat through friction; a small pile of smouldering tinder dust can form at the end of the groove. Te Ara independently describes the pointed hard-wood stick being rubbed rapidly in grooved softer wood until ignition conditions are reached.

Candidate law:
**RECIPROCAL FRICTION RELEASES LATENT HEAT INTO EMBER.**

Candidate proof:
`HIKA ≠ KAUAHI → CONTACT → FRICTION → HEAT → EMBER`

Pair-native test:
**VERY HIGH.** The two tool roles are distinct and mutually necessary for this specific fire-plough relation. The ember is an emergent result at their interface.

Third-result strength:
**VERY HIGH.** Ember / heat is not contained as a visible object in either card beforehand.

Existing-family comparison:
- not Swell: no navigation / environmental sensing;
- not Kento: no transfer/imprint;
- not Khipu: no knot/tension;
- not Fès: no fit/tessellation;
- not Ombak/Siku: no sound law;
- not Binocular: no optical disparity.

Interaction translation:
- Card A — HIKA / ACTIVE STICK
- Card B — KAUAHI / GROOVED BASE
- Gap — FRICTION / HEAT FIELD
- repeated controlled passes accumulate an editorial heat trace;
- ember appears only after both objects maintain the target contact relation;
- source objects remain distinct after registration.

Truthful Other state:
`OTHER CONTACT`
- contact path misses / does not sustain the selected groove relation;
- warmth / dust may appear editorially, but no target ember registers;
- both sticks remain valid;
- no red X.

Truthfulness boundary:
The prototype must **not** claim exact ignition temperature, wood moisture, stroke speed, timing or survival instruction. It is an editorial relation model, not a fire-starting tutorial.

Visual rights:
Authoritative Māori imagery is often reuse-restricted. Science Museum Group has a Māori fire-plough photograph under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, which is not ideal for an unrestricted portfolio. Implementation should therefore use an original source-grounded rendering / material study of the two tool forms unless a permissive Māori-specific source is found.

Cultural sensitivity:
Moderate. Hika ahi is living cultural knowledge and should be named accurately, sourced, and not aestheticised with generic “tribal” motifs. The relation itself provides the identity.

Qualification verdict:
# FINALIST

# Candidate B — Aboriginal Australia / Millstone + Muller

Pair:
- lower `MILLSTONE`
- upper `MULLER`

Authoritative mechanism:
Australian Museum identifies the larger lower platform as the millstone and the smaller upper stone as the muller. Indigenous grindstones have been used to grind seeds into flour and also to process pigments, resins and other materials.

Candidate law:
**RECIPROCAL ABRASION TURNS WHOLE MATERIAL INTO PREPARED MATTER.**

Candidate proof:
`MULLER + MILLSTONE → PRESSURE / MOTION → GRANULATION`

Pair-native test:
**HIGH.** The stones have complementary roles, but the material being processed is a necessary third participant.

Third-result strength:
HIGH. Granulated / prepared matter emerges from the relation, but the result is partly attributable to the inserted substrate rather than only the two product objects.

Existing-family comparison:
Distinct from Khipu/Fès/Kento/Ombak/Siku/Binocular. Risk of thematic overlap with Toyama Food if flour becomes the hero.

Interaction translation:
Possible central particle field accumulating between the stones.

Truthful Other state:
Different pressure/path leaves coarser material or incomplete target preparation while both stones remain valid.

Visual rights:
Australian Museum evidence is excellent but most object photography is copyrighted. An original source-grounded rendering would likely be required.

Sensitivity:
Requires care around First Nations specificity and avoiding pan-Aboriginal generalisation. A build would need a clearly named Country/community or source context rather than treating all Australian grindstone traditions as interchangeable.

Qualification verdict:
# STRONG RESERVE

Reason not finalist:
The two tools are strong, but the causal proof is more triadic than Hika Ahi and the cultural specificity burden is harder to solve cleanly in one family without over-generalising.

# Candidate C — Polynesia / Double-Hulled Canoe

Pair:
- `HULL A`
- `HULL B`

Authoritative mechanism:
Te Ara states that two hulls gave Polynesian vessels stability and seaworthiness. Te Papa describes double-hulled canoes as two hulls joined by booms and decking. Scholarly sailing-performance work likewise treats the connected double hull as a specific Pacific technological form.

Candidate law:
**DISTRIBUTED BUOYANCY PRODUCES STABILITY.**

Candidate proof:
`HULL A + HULL B → JOIN → DISTRIBUTE → STABILIZE`

Pair-native test:
**VERY HIGH.** Two large physical bodies remain distinct and are structurally coupled.

Third-result strength:
VERY HIGH. Stability / seaworthiness is an emergent system property of the coupled configuration.

Existing-family comparison:
Mechanically distinct from Swell, but the maritime/ocean context creates a perceptual overlap risk. The build must remain about **structure / coupled buoyancy / load distribution**, never navigation or swell reading.

Interaction translation:
- Card A — HULL A
- Card B — HULL B
- Gap — CROSSBEAM / COUPLING FIELD
- pair approaches parallel spacing;
- cross-connection resolves;
- center load/deck field stabilizes;
- source hulls remain visibly separate.

Truthful Other state:
`OTHER LOAD / OTHER SPACING`
- hulls remain valid;
- selected editorial load vector leaves a residual lean / instability cue;
- no claim that a historical vessel configuration is “wrong”.

Visual rights:
Strongest rights path of the four. Wikimedia Commons has a 2025 image of Polynesian double-hulled canoes licensed CC BY-SA 4.0. Historical Tasman/Turnbull visuals are source-rich but institutionally restricted for reuse.

Sensitivity:
Lower than Hika Ahi if framed as documented Pacific engineering rather than pan-Polynesian decoration. Still requires precise regional naming if a specific visual is selected.

Qualification verdict:
# FINALIST

# Candidate D — Pacific Tapa Beater + Anvil

Pair:
- tapa beater (`i‘e` / `ike`, terminology varies)
- wooden anvil (`tutua` in Tahitian example)

Authoritative mechanism:
Te Papa documents the beater used to pound and flatten inner bark on a special wooden anvil until it widens into larger barkcloth. Tapa-making is widespread across Pacific cultures with distinct local names and practices.

Candidate law:
**REPEATED COMPRESSION TURNS A STRIP INTO A SURFACE.**

Candidate proof:
`BEATER + ANVIL → IMPACT → SPREAD → SURFACE`

Pair-native test:
HIGH-MEDIUM. Beater and anvil are a genuine tool pair, but bark is a required third material.

Third-result strength:
HIGH. Widened barkcloth surface emerges through the process.

Existing-family comparison:
Distinct if framed as compression/widening. High risk of drifting toward Kento if decorative patterns or transfer become central.

Interaction translation:
Repeated vertical impact compresses a central bark strip which progressively widens.

Truthful Other state:
Insufficient / non-target impact leaves the bark strip narrower; both tools remain valid.

Visual rights:
Te Papa tool images commonly carry restrictive rights. Original renderings likely required.

Sensitivity:
Requires choosing a specific Pacific tradition rather than mixing Samoan, Tahitian, Tongan and Fijian terminology into one generic “tapa” family.

Qualification verdict:
# STRONG RESERVE

Reason not finalist:
Excellent tactile mechanism, but the relation is more triadic and the cross-cultural terminology/visual-rights problem is greater than the finalists.
